A corpus is a collection of authentic texts gathered according to explicit design criteria in order to study a language or a language variety. Corpus design begins with a decision about the population that the corpus is meant to represent. A general corpus aims to cover a language as a whole, whereas a specialised corpus is restricted to a domain, a genre or a group of speakers. Representativeness is the extent to which a sample includes the full range of variability of the population. The sampling frame is the list of texts from which the sample is drawn, and a balanced corpus distributes its texts across text types in proportions that follow the design criteria.

Corpus size is measured in tokens. The number of different word forms is the number of types, and the type-token ratio relates both figures, although the type-token ratio depends heavily on corpus size. A small specialised corpus can be representative of a narrow domain, while a general corpus needs many millions of tokens.

A reference corpus is a large general corpus used as a point of comparison. A monitor corpus is updated regularly so that it can track changes in the language, while a static corpus is closed once the sampling frame has been covered. A parallel corpus contains source texts aligned with their translations, and a comparable corpus contains original texts in two or more languages that share the same design criteria. A learner corpus collects texts produced by language learners, and a spoken corpus contains transcriptions of recorded speech. Whatever the type, corpus design must be documented, because the design criteria determine which conclusions can be drawn from the corpus.
