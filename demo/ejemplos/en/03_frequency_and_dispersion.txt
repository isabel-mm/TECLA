Frequency is the most basic statistic in corpus linguistics. A frequency list ranks the word forms or the lemmas of a corpus by their number of occurrences. Raw frequency is the number of times that a word occurs in a corpus. Because corpora differ in size, raw frequency is usually converted into normalised frequency, which is expressed per million tokens. Normalised frequency allows the comparison of a small specialised corpus with a large reference corpus.

Word frequency follows a very skewed frequency distribution: a few function words are extremely frequent, while a large proportion of the types occur only once. A word that occurs only once in a corpus is a hapax legomenon. This frequency distribution explains why the type-token ratio falls as corpus size grows, and why a frequency list always has a long tail of rare words.

Frequency alone can be misleading. Dispersion describes how evenly a word is distributed across the texts of a corpus. A dispersion measure complements raw frequency, because a word may be frequent only in a single text. Document frequency, the number of texts in which a word occurs, is the simplest dispersion measure. A word with a high normalised frequency and a poor dispersion is typical of one author or one topic, and not of the language variety that the corpus represents.

Frequency lists are also the starting point of vocabulary growth studies. A vocabulary growth curve plots the number of types against the number of tokens, and it shows whether a specialised corpus is large enough to cover the vocabulary of its domain.
