Abstract: Automatic Term Extraction (ATE) is generally framed as a super-
vised sequence-labelling task, in which models learn to identify terminological units
from annotated examples. The rise of neural architectures has driven significant ad-
vancements, often relegating the role of linguistic features to a secondary position,
despite their being essential for describing the nature and behavior of terminologi-
cal units. This work presents a model based on Conditional Random Fields (CRF)
enriched with linguistic and statistical features and designed to model the termino-
logical unit beyond superficial lexical forms. To distinguish between memorization
and genuine terminological competence, we adopt a strict term-exclusion protocol.
Using sentence-level cross-validation, we ensure that each test partition contains
unseen terms, allowing us to evaluate the model’s capacity to generalize to new
units. The results demonstrate that, under this strict evaluation, the integration of
linguistic features is fundamental, achieving a Macro F1 of ∼ 0.79. Furthermore,
inspecting the CRF weights allows us to interpret the model’s terminological com-
petence, explicitly identifying which linguistic and statistical features condition or
determine termhood. Collectively, this proposal offers a transparent and replicable
methodology for ATE, focused on interpretability and generalization over unseen
lexicon.
Keywords: Automatic Term Extraction, Terminology, Termhood, Machine Learn-

1 Introduction

Automatic Term Extraction (ATE) is a cen-
tral task in domain–specific Natural Lan-
guage Processing (Jacquemin and Bouri-
gault, 2005; Astrakhantsev, Fedorenko, and
Turdakov, 2015; Heylen and De
Her-
tog, 2015) aimed at identifying lexical units
that encode specialised concepts and sup-
port the organisation and transmission of ex-
pert knowledge (Cabr´e, 1999; Estop`a, 1999;
Stankovi´c et al., 2016). Over the past
decades, ATE has evolved from early sta-
tistical approaches based on frequency and
association measures to linguistically driven
rule-based methods, and more recently to su-
pervised sequence-labelling architectures, in-
creasingly implemented through neural mod-
els (Astrakhantsev, Fedorenko, and Tur-
dakov, 2015; Lossio-Ventura et al., 2016;
Tran et al., 2023; Bland´on Andrade et al.,
2025).

As these approaches increasingly shape
ATE research, evaluation becomes a cor-
nerstone of methodological design (Bernier-
Colborne, 2012; Terryn et al., 2020). How-
ever, assessing performance in ATE is not
merely a matter of measuring predictive ac-
it must reflect how terminology be-
curacy:
haves in text. Thus, an evaluation proto-
col that ignores this risks rewarding systems
for recovering familiar lexical items rather
than identifying the linguistic properties that
distinguish terms from general vocabulary.
This issue has already been observed in re-
lated NLP tasks where models achieve decep-
tively high performance by exploiting super-
ficial lexical overlap instead of learning ab-
stract linguistic relations (Levy et al., 2015;
Babl et al., 2025). ATE is especially vulner-
able to this issue because terminological lexi-
cons are highly uneven and relatively limited
in variety in contrast to the rest of lexical
units, i.e., once a term enters specialised dis-
course, it tends to recur frequently, which ul-
timately makes it difficult to distinguish gen-
uine terminological competence from lexical
familiarity.

This paper examines whether a supervised
ATE model can infer termhood from linguis-
tic and quantitative evidence in specialised
discourse rather than relying on prior expo-
sure to particular lexical forms. To address
this question, we adopt an evaluation pro-
tocol that removes any lexical overlap be-
tween training and test sets in order to assess

whether the model can identify terms it has
never encountered during training.

RQ: Do supervised ATE models gen-
uinely generalise to unseen terms, or do con-
ventional evaluation practices simply reward
lexical memorisation?

From this question we derive two hypothe-

ses:

1. Performance in conventional ATE set-
tings is overestimated due to lexical
overlap between training and test par-
titions.

2. When such overlap is prevented, a model
that exploits linguistic, contextual and
distributional cues associated with ter-
mhood should maintain stable perfor-
mance.

Based on these hypotheses, our study pur-

sues three objectives:

1. Evaluate a Conditional Random Field
(CRF) model for ATE under controlled
cross-validation scenarios.

2. Implement a term-exclusion protocol en-
suring strict separation of term types be-
tween training and test sets.

3. Establish a

reproducible

evaluation
framework that distinguishes memorisa-
tion from genuine terminological infer-
ence.

Yet ATE inherits a foundational problem

These questions persist because, although
the judgement of whether a lexical unit
functions as a term is ultimately pragmatic
and context-dependent—at least from a lin-
guistically oriented perspective, as opposed
to the foundational settings of Terminol-
ogy that conceived terms as stable, pre-
defined units (Cabr´e, 1999)—terms do not
systematically exhibit overt formal mark-
ers that disclose their specialised status.
Nonetheless, their use in specialised discourse
leaves identifiable traces—morphosyntactic
regularities, distributional profiles and con-
textual patterns—that signal their epistemic
role (L’Homme, 2020). To operationalise
this distinction, Computational Terminology
introduces two complementary constructs
(Kageura and Umino, 1996):

1. unithood, or the degree to which a se-
quence forms a stable linguistic unit, and

2. termhood, or the extent to which that
unit is conceptually salient within a spe-
cialised domain.

While the first captures syntagmatic co-
hesion—e.g.
noun-headedness and modi-
fier–head configurations—the second reflects

domain relevance and epistemic centrality
(Nazar, 2016). These constructs underpin
the dominant computational formulation of
ATE as a two-phase pipeline (Vivaldi and
Rodr´ıguez, 2011; Ananiadou and McNaught,
2005; Kockaert et al., 2015): (i) candidate ex-
traction, which retrieves potential units using
linguistic templates or n-gram heuristics, and
(ii) candidate validation, which determines
which candidates qualify as terms via statis-
tical evidence or supervised learning (Tran
et al., 2024). More recent neural approaches
learn these distinctions implicitly, but often
conflate terminological inference with lexical
memorisation, leaving unresolved the ques-
tion of whether models can infer what makes
a lexical unit a term rather than merely
recognise previously seen forms.

Despite the rise of neural architectures
and Transformer-based embeddings, which
infer contextual semantic properties without
explicit feature design, CRFs remain rele-
vant for ATE due to their interpretability and
their suitability for low-resource conditions.
CRFs make explicit how termhood emerges
from observable linguistic evidence—a dis-
tinction central to this study (Zheng, Zhao,
and Yang, 2009). Rather than relying on
massive pretraining or implicit representa-
tions, the present work employs a CRF en-
riched with morphosyntactic, orthographic,
distributional and contextual features, en-
abling a controlled examination of whether
such signals are sufficient for modelling termi-
nological competence. Feature-based CRFs
therefore constitute an appropriate testbed
for isolating the contribution of
linguistic
and quantitative cues to the inference of ter-
mhood. This motivates the methodologi-
cal choice adopted here: evaluating a single
CRF architecture under a controlled term-
exclusion protocol to determine whether a
model can infer termhood for previously un-
seen lexical forms.

3 Methodology

This section details the methodological de-
sign adopted to assess ATE as a supervised
sequence-labelling task. Our approach is
grounded in two complementary principles.
First, we model ATE following a three-class
BIO labelling scheme (§ 3.1). Second, the
experimental setup is applied to a domain-
specific corpus (§ 3.2), which constitutes the
basis for feature-selection, training (§ 3.3)
and evaluation (§ 3.4).

3.1 Task
ATE can be conceptualised as a supervised
classification task applied at the token level,
in which each lexical item receives a label in-
dicating its participation in a domain-specific
term.
This classification can be opera-
tionalised in two ways: categorically, through
discrete decisions that assert whether a token
belongs to a term, or along a continuum that
assigns degrees of terminicity (Nazar, 2016).
In this experiment, ATE is implemented as
a supervised sequence labelling problem in
which each token in a sentence receives a
label marking whether it participates in a
domain-specific term. Each sentence in the
corpus is represented as a sequence of tokens
paired with BIO labels. Multiword terms are
annotated by marking the first token as (B-T)
(beginning of term) and subsequent tokens as
(I-T) (inside term), while all tokens that do
not belong to a term are labelled as outside
of the term (O).

This scheme allows for the preservation
of the internal structure and boundaries of
both single- and multiword terms, while still
understanding ATE as a sequence-labelling
task. The resulting representation provides
an explicit training signal that allows the su-
pervised model to observe the linguistic envi-
ronments in which terms occurs. Under this
formulation, the core problem is to decide,
for each token in context, whether it initiates,
continues or does not participate in a termi-
nological unit within specialised discourse.

3.2 Dataset
The dataset used in this study consists of a
domain-specific corpus in Corpus Linguistics,
compiled with the aim of capturing the con-
ceptual and terminological repertoire char-
acteristic of this field. The corpus includes
50 full-text scientific texts published in spe-
cialised venues, selected according to three

criteria: (i) thematic relevance, ensuring that
the texts discuss core topics such as corpus
design, annotation, representativeness, quan-
titative methods and linguistic description
from a corpus-based perspective; (ii) disci-
plinary homogeneity, restricting the corpus
to texts authored within the field to main-
tain conceptual coherence; and (iii) textual
completeness, privileging full articles over ab-
stracts or sections to preserve terminological
context. Only publications from 2015 on-
wards were included, ensuring that the cor-
pus reflects contemporary practices and the
current terminological landscape of the dis-
cipline (K¨yto, Rudanko, and Smitterberg,
2000; Dur´an Mu˜noz, 2011).

lexical separation between training and test
data. Rather than randomly splitting at the
sentence level, we implement a strict lexical
cross-validation procedure that operates on
complete term types.

teraction of linguistic and domain-sensitive
statistical features, providing a principled ba-
sis for hybrid neural–CRF architectures in fu-
ture research.

5 Discussion

The CRF-based ATE model achieved a
macro F1 of approximately 0.79 under a rig-
orous evaluation setting. This indicates a
modest overall ability to separate terms from
other lexical units in text, despite the chal-
lenging constraints imposed by the evalua-
tion protocol. To interpret this performance
in detail, we consider the class-specific re-
sults for the terminological labels (B/I-T). In
practical terms, the system is conservative in
initiating terms – it rarely flags a token as
a term beginning unless there is strong evi-
dence, leading to few false term starts (hence
the high precision). This caution is benefi-
cial because a spurious term start would yield
an incorrect term extraction. On the other
hand, the model shows high recall for I-T la-
bels, suggesting that once a term has begun,
the model is adept at capturing the remaining
tokens of the term. In other words, it tends to
label interior tokens of multi-word terms cor-
rectly, seldom leaving out tokens that should
be part of a term. This implies the CRF has
learned strong sequential patterns for contin-
uing terms, likely leveraging context to de-
cide that a token belongs inside an existing
term. The trade-off is that precision for I-T
might be lower than its recall – the model
sometimes labels a token as inside a term

when it is not, potentially over-extending a
term boundary. Such behavior aligns with
the known importance of boundary detection
ATE, where even advanced language models
struggle with precise term span identification
(Chun et al., 2025)).

A key aspect of our evaluation was a
strict term-exclusion cross-validation proto-
col, wherein no term is present in both the
training and test sets. This means the model
was never evaluated on a term it had seen
during training, eliminating any chance to
simply memorize term appearances. Such
a protocol provides a stringent test of gen-
eralization. By prohibiting lexical overlap,
we ensure that the model’s performance re-
flects its ability to recognize novel terms
based on learned patterns, rather than re-
calling known terms from training data as
a result of lexical overlapping (Chun et al.,
2025). The relevance of this protocol be-
comes evident when contextualised within
the broader literature. As has been re-
peatedly noted, despite shared reliance on
precision, recall and F1-scores, ATE eval-
uation lacks methodological standardisation
(Bernier-Colborne, 2012; Rigouts Terryn,
Hoste, and Lefever, 2022). Studies differ in
the construction of their gold standards, the
minimum and maximum lengths of allowed
terms, the treatment of MWT sequences, and
whether partial matches are accepted as cor-
rect or not. These inconsistencies preclude
direct comparison of reported scores and have
contributed to a fragmented view of system
performance in the field (Terryn et al., 2020;
Rigouts Terryn, Hoste, and Lefever, 2022).
The suggested model is consistent with
other supervised approaches (Zheng, Zhao,
and Yang, 2009; Zhang, Song, and Fang,
2010).
It’s worth noting that some re-
cent studies using deep neural models (e.g.,
BERT fine-tuning or BiLSTM-CRF with em-
beddings) have pushed performance slightly
higher on certain benchmarks (Zhang, Zhou,
and Li, 2020; Chun et al., 2025). However,
those gains often come at the cost of requir-
ing much larger training data and the risk
of losing some interpretability. Adittionally,
our work is aligned with research highlight-
ing the continued relevance of explicit linguis-
tic knowledge in ATE systems (Tran et al.,
2023). We incorporate many of these cues
within a learning framework and complement
indica-
them with corpus-driven statistical

tors. However, the language-agnostic nature
of several of our features (e.g., frequency-
based measures and general POS categories)
raises the question of how the approach might
scale beyond English.
Some cues—such
as suffixal patterns and prototypical multi-
word term structures—are language-specific,
which suggests that adaptations will be re-
quired for morphologically richer or analyt-
ically different languages. The broader lit-
erature acknowledges that multilingual su-
pervised ATE remains constrained by the
scarcity of domain-diverse, high-quality ter-
minological corpora (Bernier-Colborne, 2012;
Rigouts Terryn, Hoste, and Lefever, 2022).
such as the ACTER
Existing resources,
dataset (Terryn et al., 2020), constitute an
important step forward, but coverage is still
limited in terms of both languages and spe-
cialised domains. This bottleneck partly ex-
plains why most supervised ATE research
focuses on English and why generalisation
across domains still remains an unresolved
challenge.

6 Conclusion and Future Work

This study has demonstrated that ATE is
a task which can be modelled as a princi-
pled inference task rather than a memorisa-
tion exercise, provided that evaluation pro-
tocols enforce true lexical independence be-
tween training and testing data. The be-
haviour of the system offers a nuanced view
of terminological structure. Its conservative
approach to detecting term onsets leads to
high precision for initial term labels, while its
strong sequential modelling capabilities en-
sure high recall for interior components of
MWT, which highlights the long-established
complexity of boundary delimitation in ATE:
although the internal cohesion of terms is well
captured, determining their exact span re-
mains a challenging aspect of the task, which
is ultimately intrinsic to the nature of termi-
nological units (§ 3.1).

Moreover, the characteristics of termino-
logical corpora themselves contribute to this
difficulty. As discussed in the dataset analy-
sis, specialised corpora are highly repetitive
and saturated with recurrent lexical patterns
and morphological variants (§ 3.2). The fact
that the model achieves competitive results
under strict lexical separation reinforces the
conclusion that its performance is not merely
an artefact of corpus repetition, but evidence

of genuine generalisation over systematic en-
coded features of termhood.

Beyond performance, the results aim to
contribute methodological clarity to a field
often characterised by heterogeneous evalua-
tion practices (Bernier-Colborne, 2012; Ter-
ryn et al., 2020; Rigouts Terryn, Hoste, and
Lefever, 2022). Overall, the findings invite
a reconsideration of how success in ATE is
measured and achieved. The evidence pre-
sented here indicates that models capable of
leveraging linguistic cues can extract novel
terminology reliably, provided that evalua-
tion frameworks are aligned with that ob-
jective.
In this sense, the contribution lies
not only in the performance of the model it-
self, but in suggesting conditions under which
such performance can be meaningfully inter-
preted and compared.

Future work will explore how these in-
sights may be extended through hybrid ar-
chitectures that combine the interpretability
of feature-based models with the representa-
tional capacity of neural contextual embed-
dings (Zhang, Zhou, and Li, 2020). Such
integration may alleviate boundary-detection
errors by capturing semantic regularities that
exceed the scope of handcrafted features
alone. Additionally, further research will ex-
amine alternative evaluation scenarios,
in-
cluding fuzzy similarity-based protocols that
account for partial or near-equivalent term
matches. These scenarios may better reflect
the gradience inherent in terminological vari-
ation, offering a more nuanced view of system
performance beyond strict span identity.
